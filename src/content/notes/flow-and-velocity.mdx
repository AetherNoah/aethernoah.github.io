The first useful distinction is between a model of motion through a probability distribution and the objective used to improve that model. A velocity field describes the former. A reward-based training procedure changes the latter.

## Begin with a path

Take a data sample $x_1$ and a noise sample $x_0$. A simple interpolation is

$$
x_t = (1-t)x_0 + tx_1, \qquad t \in [0,1].
$$

Along this conditional path, the target velocity is $x_1-x_0$. A flow-matching objective can therefore be written as

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,x_0,x_1}\left[\left\|v_\theta(x_t,t)-(x_1-x_0)\right\|^2\right].
$$

This is a regression objective for a velocity field. It is not, by itself, a reward objective.[^path]

<aside className="sidenote">Margin note — This interpolation runs from noise at t = 0 to data at t = 1. Some implementations reverse the time convention.</aside>

## From a field to a sample

Sampling approximately integrates $dx_t/dt=v_\theta(x_t,t)$. A minimal Euler update makes the role of the field concrete:

```python
def euler_step(x, t, dt, velocity_model):
    velocity = velocity_model(x, t)
    return x + dt * velocity
```

Reducing the step size changes numerical error. Changing the learned velocity changes the distribution we are trying to sample. These are different interventions.

## Where the reward enters

Group-relative optimization compares rewards within a group of generated samples. An illustrative normalized advantage is

$$
A_i = \frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(r)}{\operatorname{std}(r)+\epsilon}.
$$

The hard part is not computing this number. It is specifying a sampling process with a tractable policy likelihood, then relating that likelihood to the model’s parameters. A deterministic ODE update does not automatically provide a stochastic transition likelihood.

> Keep the representation of the generator, the sampling process, and the optimization objective separate until their connection is explicit.
